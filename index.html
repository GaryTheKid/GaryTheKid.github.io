My Story
My name is Chongyang Wang, usually folks call me Gary! I'm a PhD student in Computer Science at Northeastern University and a research assistant in the Relational Agents Group with Prof. Timothy W. Bickmore. I build and study AI agents that teach: embodied conversational agents, virtual reality, and LLM-driven tutoring, mostly for patient and health education and increasingly for education in general.
AI has driven the cost of telling to zero. It has not lowered the cost of learning.
That one judgment runs through all my work. Today's models can tell you almost anything, often better than most people, yet none of them can take a person from not knowing to actually knowing, start to finish, on its own: the whole job of a competent tutor. I split that gap into four layers, one for each part of a tutor's job: the content, the learner, the tutor-learner relationship, and institutions. You can watch the whole argument in the video below, then walk through it node by node in the Problem Map.
The full story, 28 minWhy AI Still Can't Teach on Its Own
Every node in the Problem Map below also has a ▶ Watch this part button that jumps straight to its moment in the video.
My PhD work tackles the first three layers with patients: Dr. Clara, an LLM-driven agent that walks patients through their education materials in VR; a pedagogical layer that takes teaching decisions out of the language model; teach-back, where the agent checks whether anything actually stuck; and life-sized VR health coaches, whose rapport and physical distance shape how people respond. Outside campus I build learning tools with K-12 teachers (AI PBL Agent, Keyi) and teach middle schoolers to make games with AI (First Principle Agent). That is where I learned the fourth layer.
Before Northeastern I was at Columbia University. In Prof. Joey Lee's TC Game Research Lab I learned that a well-designed game is a well-designed learning experience, and in Prof. Steven Feiner's CGUI Lab I worked on XR and 3D interfaces for NSF-funded human-robot collaboration in future manufacturing (ACM SUI 2023).
Teaching and making have always gone together for me. As an instructional designer I authored official Unity Learn courses and ran game-development workshops, and I still design and build games, these days a card tactics roguelike called For Boaria!. That mix is why I care less about what an AI can say and more about what a learner walks away with.

The Problem Map
AI tells you things very well, often better than most people. What it cannot yet do is teach: do the whole job of a competent tutor and carry one person from not knowing to knowing, start to finish. This map is how I take that gap apart. It is also the architecture of my research: understand the material, understand the learner, deliver in real time, plus a fourth layer I only saw by building things outside the lab. Click any node.

Why this is worth the long haul

Suppose AI becomes a hundred times stronger. What must a person still do for themselves? Make the decisions about their own life: whether to take this drug, have this operation, believe this piece of news. To decide, you have to understand at least a little, so human cognition cannot be fully outsourced, and people must keep the ability to learn.

The most effective teaching we know, one-on-one and personalized, is also the scarcest, so it goes to those who can pay or know the right people. An AI that can truly teach could fill that gap, but only if it gets past telling. For most people the bottleneck was never a shortage of material; it is motivation, trust, and whether they can keep going. Flipped over, the map becomes four design goals:

EfficientMore of what is taught actually stays, per minute of learning.
Low painThe effort of learning can be reduced, never removed.
Stable over timeNot the novelty of one session, but months and years of company.
Through a narrow channelStill leaving a trace through text, voice and a screen.
